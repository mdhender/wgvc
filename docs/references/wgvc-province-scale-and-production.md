# WGVC Province Scale and Raw Production

## Purpose

This document establishes a working physical scale for provinces generated by **wgvc** and defines a model for representing their raw productive potential.

The goal is to keep geography and economic capacity separate:

- a province has a physical area;
- terrain, climate, water, soils, forests, and geology determine what that area can sustainably produce;
- productive capacity is expressed using resource-appropriate units rather than treating every acre as equivalent;
- population and economic systems can be layered on top later by TNYC or another consumer of the generated world.

Luxury goods are intentionally excluded from this first model. They can be added later using concepts similar to the GURPS *Realm Management* rules.

---

## 1. Province Scale

### Wilderness hex

Use the traditional **six-mile wilderness hex**:

- 6 miles from face to opposite face
- apothem: 3 miles
- area of a regular hex:

```text
A = 2 × sqrt(3) × apothem²
  = 2 × sqrt(3) × 3²
  ≈ 31.177 square miles
```

Since one square mile contains 640 acres:

```text
31.177 × 640 ≈ 19,953 acres
```

Therefore one six-mile wilderness hex represents approximately:

- **31.18 square miles**
- **19,950 acres**
- **80.75 km²**

### Average province

Set the target average province size to the equivalent of **seven wilderness hexes**.

```text
7 × 31.177 ≈ 218.24 square miles
7 × 19,953 ≈ 139,670 acres
```

For design purposes:

> **One average province is approximately 218 square miles, or 140,000 acres.**

Seven compact hexes correspond roughly to the familiar one-center-plus-six-neighbors arrangement. A province of this area is therefore on the order of 20–25 miles across, depending on its shape.

A typical TNYC world containing 10,000 provinces consequently represents approximately:

- 70,000 six-mile wilderness hexes
- 2.18 million square miles
- 5.65 million km²

The actual generated provinces need not all have identical areas. Seven wilderness hexes is the **mean physical scale**.

## 2. The Hide as Agricultural Capacity

A medieval English **hide** was not consistently a fixed physical area. It evolved into a unit associated with productive and fiscal capacity. A conventional nominal value of approximately **120 acres** is useful for the game model.

A perfectly productive average province would therefore contain the equivalent of:

```text
139,670 acres / 120 acres per hide ≈ 1,164 hides
```

Thus:

> **The theoretical maximum agricultural carrying capacity of an average province is approximately 1,164 equivalent hides.**

This does **not** mean that the province is divided into 1,164 literal 120-acre parcels.

Instead, the hide is a unit of **agricultural carrying capacity**.

For example, 120 acres of excellent farmland might provide roughly one hide of capacity, while considerably more acreage of marginal land might be required to provide the same productive capacity. Mountains, desert, marsh, barren rock, dense wilderness, and other unsuitable land may contribute little or no agricultural capacity.

A province's agricultural capacity can therefore be represented as:

```text
Agricultural CC = ideal hide capacity × agricultural productivity
```

For an average-sized province:

```text
Agricultural CC ≈ 1,164 × agricultural productivity
```

where agricultural productivity is normally in the range 0.0–1.0.

Examples:

| Productivity | Agricultural CC |
|---:|---:|
| 100% | 1,164 hides |
| 75% | 873 hides |
| 50% | 582 hides |
| 25% | 291 hides |
| 10% | 116 hides |
| 5% | 58 hides |
| 1% | 12 hides |

This allows provinces of identical physical area to have radically different human carrying capacities.

If a later rule establishes that one agricultural hide can support **N people**, then the agricultural population ceiling is simply:

```text
Population Capacity = Agricultural CC × N
```

The value of **N** should be established separately from the geographic model.

## 3. Carrying Capacity Is Multidimensional

Agricultural carrying capacity should not be the province's only measure of productive potential.

A province can independently possess capacity for:

- agriculture;
- forestry;
- fishing and other aquatic resources;
- grazing/pastoralism;
- mineral extraction.

These capacities do **not** need to sum to 100 percent.

A forested river valley, for example, might simultaneously have substantial agricultural, forestry, and fishing capacity. A mountainous province might have very little agricultural capacity while possessing extensive forests, grazing land, and valuable mineral deposits.

This suggests a general model:

```text
Province
    physical area
    agricultural capacity
    pastoral capacity
    forestry capacity
    fishery capacity
    mineral resources
```

The physical province is the container. The capacities describe what that geography can produce.

## 4. Renewable Capacity Versus Exhaustible Resources

Not every raw resource should use the same mechanics.

### Renewable carrying capacities

Agriculture, grazing, forestry, and fishing are fundamentally **renewable** resources when exploitation remains within sustainable limits.

They can therefore be represented as carrying capacities: an amount that can be produced repeatedly without permanently consuming the underlying resource.

Capacity can change because of:

- clearing;
- irrigation;
- drainage;
- roads and infrastructure;
- improved agricultural technology;
- climate changes;
- erosion;
- fire;
- war and devastation;
- overgrazing;
- deforestation;
- overfishing.

Some activities naturally trade one capacity for another. Clearing forest may increase agricultural or pastoral capacity while reducing forestry capacity.

### Exhaustible resources

Minerals are different.

An ore body is primarily a **stock**, not a renewable carrying capacity. A mine extracts material from a finite reserve.

Mineral resources should therefore eventually distinguish at least:

```text
resource type
quality / grade
accessible reserve
extraction difficulty
```

Technology may make previously inaccessible deposits economically useful, but extraction normally reduces the remaining reserve.

This distinction should remain visible in the model even if early implementations use simplified numbers.

## 5. Recommended Raw Production Goods

The initial goods list should be small enough to make world generation and economic simulation understandable. The generator should describe fundamental resources rather than enumerate every crop, tree species, fish species, or mineral.

### Agriculture

**Grain**  
Staple cereal production: wheat, barley, rye, oats, millet, rice, maize, or regional equivalents. Grain is the primary storable food staple.

**Produce**  
Vegetables, fruits, legumes, roots, and other cultivated food crops that are not conveniently represented as grain.

**Fiber**  
Plant fibers and similar agricultural materials used for cloth, cordage, and related production: flax, hemp, cotton, and regional equivalents.

### Pastoral production

**Livestock**  
Animals raised primarily for meat, milk, hides, wool, traction, and breeding. Individual animal types can be represented later where necessary.

This should have its own pastoral/grazing capacity rather than being treated as ordinary cropland.

### Forestry

**Timber**  
Construction-quality wood: beams, planks, ship timber, and other durable structural material.

**Wood**  
Lower-grade wood used primarily as fuel, charcoal feedstock, fencing, tools, and ordinary domestic material.

Separating timber from ordinary wood allows a heavily wooded province to be useful without implying that all forests contain abundant ship- or construction-quality trees.

### Aquatic resources

**Fish**  
Fish, shellfish, and other significant food harvested from rivers, lakes, wetlands, and coastal waters.

The province's fishery capacity can incorporate both freshwater and marine productivity unless the simulation later needs to distinguish them.

### Mineral resources

For the first version, use a small set of economically distinct raw mineral classes rather than individual ores.

**Iron Ore**  
The fundamental ferrous-metal resource.

**Copper Ore**  
Copper-bearing ores. Tin can initially be modeled separately because of its particular importance to bronze production and trade.

**Tin Ore**  
Tin-bearing ores.

**Lead Ore**  
Lead-bearing ores; silver-bearing lead deposits can later produce silver as a secondary or luxury resource.

**Stone**  
Useful quarried building stone. This represents workable construction material rather than ordinary exposed rock.

**Salt**  
Mine, spring, or evaporative salt production. Salt deserves explicit treatment because it is a basic necessity, preservative, and major preindustrial trade commodity.

### Recommended initial list

The resulting core raw-goods vocabulary is deliberately short:

```text
Grain
Produce
Fiber
Livestock
Timber
Wood
Fish
Iron Ore
Copper Ore
Tin Ore
Lead Ore
Stone
Salt
```

Thirteen raw goods are enough to create meaningful geographic differences without requiring the world generator to become an agricultural or geological encyclopedia.

## 6. Why Not Add More Goods Yet?

Many historically important resources could be modeled separately:

- horses;
- cattle;
- sheep;
- wool;
- leather;
- clay;
- peat;
- coal;
- gold;
- silver;
- gems;
- spices;
- wine;
- olive oil;
- silk;
- dyes;
- medicinal plants;
- furs.

They should not necessarily be part of the base raw-production model.

Some are **processed goods** rather than raw resources. Others are highly regional resources whose principal game function is scarcity and trade rather than subsistence.

Those are good candidates for the later **luxury/special goods** system inspired by GURPS *Realm Management*.

The core list should answer the more fundamental question:

> What ordinary productive resources does this piece of geography provide?

Luxury and special resources can answer:

> What unusual thing makes this province economically or strategically valuable?

## 7. Example Province

A generated province might eventually look conceptually like this:

```text
Province 4f2a...

Area:                 142,300 acres
Agricultural CC:      310 hides
Pastoral CC:          520 units
Forestry CC:          760 units
Fishery CC:           85 units

Mineral Resources:
    Iron Ore:         moderate deposit
    Copper Ore:       none
    Tin Ore:          small deposit
    Lead Ore:         none
    Stone:            abundant
    Salt:             none
```

The province's actual economy is a separate concern. A wilderness province may possess enormous productive potential while producing almost nothing because nobody lives there or because its inhabitants lack the technology, labor, capital, or infrastructure needed to exploit it.

This separation is intentional:

```text
WGVC geography -> potential capacity
TNYC population + technology + investment -> actual production
```

## 8. Design Principles

1. **Area and productivity are separate.** A large province is not automatically a productive province.
2. **Seven wilderness hexes is the target mean province area**, not a requirement that every province have identical size.
3. **Agricultural capacity is measured in equivalent hides**, with approximately 1,164 hides representing the theoretical agricultural maximum for an average province.
4. **A hide is a capacity measure, not necessarily a literal 120-acre parcel.**
5. **Different resource systems have independent capacities.** Productive forest does not inherently reduce fishing capacity, for example.
6. **Land use may create tradeoffs.** Clearing forest can exchange forestry capacity for agricultural or pastoral capacity.
7. **Renewable resources and exhaustible resources behave differently.** Agriculture, pasture, forestry, and fisheries have sustainable carrying capacities; mineral deposits have reserves.
8. **WGVC should generate potential, not an economy.** Population, exploitation, improvements, trade, and production belong in the consuming game system.
9. **Keep the initial raw-goods vocabulary small.** Add detail only when it creates meaningful gameplay.
10. **Luxury and special goods are a later layer.** They should make particular provinces distinctive without complicating the basic subsistence/resource model.

---

## 9. Open Questions

The next design decisions are:

1. How many people can one agricultural hide sustainably support at the baseline technology level?
2. What units should represent pastoral, forestry, and fishery carrying capacity?
3. Should those units be normalized so that an ideal average province also has a maximum near 1,164, or should each resource have a domain-specific scale?
4. How should climate and terrain combine to determine each carrying capacity?
5. How should land-use conversion work, particularly forest -> pasture -> cropland?
6. Which mineral resources deserve explicit finite reserves in the first implementation?
7. At what layer should roads, settlements, and other infrastructure modify usable capacity or actual production?
